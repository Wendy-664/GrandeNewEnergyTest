Where the heat actually is: 90 minutes of thermal soak, component by component

Everyone blames the driver IC. On our platform the IC is the cool part — the transformer winding runs 20 °C hotter. Here is the full thermal map, and what it means when the ambient is 45 °C instead of 32 °C.

Grande New Energy engineering7 min readSource: 18-page lab report

Why thermal is the whole ballgame

Electrolytic capacitor life halves for every 10 °C of extra temperature. Transformer insulation degrades on the same curve. A luminaire that measures perfectly in a 25 °C lab and runs in a 45 °C warehouse has already spent its margin before it ships.

So the useful question is not "what is your efficiency" but "which component gets hot first, and how much headroom is left". That requires a soak test at real ambient, with thermocouples on every heat source — not a single infrared snapshot after two minutes.

Test method

  • Ambient: 32 °C (100 W high bay), 33 °C (50 W flood light)
  • Duration: 90 minutes at full load, until temperatures stabilised
  • Conditions: three line voltages — 176 Vac, 230 Vac, 265 Vac
  • Points: fuse resistors, transformer wire and core, driver ICs, bridge rectifier, bus capacitor, LED board

The headline finding: the winding, not the IC

At 230 Vac the 100 W high bay sits at 82.5 °C on both driver ICs — comfortable. The EE16 transformer winding is at 100.6 °C, and at 265 Vac it climbs to 104.3 °C. That is the real thermal constraint in this design.

Why the IC stays cool

The driver IC integrates the high-voltage start-up circuit and the power switch, and it carries no magnetic energy storage. The EE16 transformer does the energy transfer, so its copper loss and core loss scale with throughput — and higher line voltage means more core loss and less copper loss, which is why the winding peaks at 265 Vac rather than at 176 Vac.

At low line, a different component wins

Drop to 176 Vac and the ranking flips. Input current roughly doubles, so the series fuse resistors become the hot spot: 100.1 °C on FR4, while the winding actually cools to 98.4 °C. Any supplier who only reports one voltage has measured half of the problem.

The 50 W flood light: same story, more margin

The smaller platform shows identical behaviour with about 10 °C more headroom across the board.

What happens at 45 °C ambient

The test was run at 32 °C, which is realistic for a shaded test cell but optimistic for a warehouse roof in July. Applying the measured rise above ambient as a first-order estimate:

Transformer winding T1 (265 Vac)104.3 °C72.3 K117.3 °C
Transformer core T1 (265 Vac)94.4 °C62.4 K107.4 °C

117 °C on a Class B (130 °C) insulation system leaves roughly 13 K of margin at 45 °C ambient. That is workable, but it is not a design you can put into a sealed, painted, unventilated housing and forget. The margin is real but it is finite, and it is spent on the transformer — not on the semiconductor everyone worries about.

Practical implications

  1. Mount with the driver cavity vertical, not horizontal. The housing is the heatsink; orientation changes internal convection more than any other variable.
  2. Never paint or coat the housing fins. A decorative powder coat on the outside adds roughly 5–8 K to every component.
  3. Leave the breather valve clear. Sealed luminaires pull moisture in through the cable gland when they cool; the valve is what stops condensation forming on the driver.
  4. Do not compare efficiency figures alone. A driver that is 92% efficient but has its losses concentrated in one small transformer will run hotter than a 90% design with distributed losses.
  5. Ask for the voltage at which the thermal test was run. A thermal report at 230 Vac only tells you about half the operating envelope.

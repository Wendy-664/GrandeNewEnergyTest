Why 100–440 Vac input matters — and what 22 measured points actually show

Most LED drivers sold into Southeast Asia are rated 180–265 Vac. Ours are measured from 100 Vac to 440 Vac, point by point, and published. Here is the raw behaviour, including the parts most suppliers would rather not show you.

Grande New Energy engineering8 min readSource: 18-page lab report

The grid you actually get is not 230 Vac

In much of Southeast Asia and South Asia, a nominal 220–240 Vac supply spends a lot of its life somewhere else. Feeder voltage sags when air-conditioning load peaks; it swells at night when industrial load drops; motor starts and welding equipment drag it down for a few cycles. A driver designed only for 220–240 Vac hands you flicker, dropped luminaires, or a field failure in year two.

The usual fix in a tender document is one line: input voltage 100–440 Vac. That line is easy to write and hard to honour. It is also easy to fake — many datasheets copy it without publishing where the driver stops regulating, where it starts protecting itself, and what it does between those two points.

What this guide shows

Two production designs — a 100 W UFO high bay and a 50 W flood light on the same ye-01 platform — measured at 22 input voltages from 100 to 440 Vac. Efficiency never drops below 88.5% in the operating window, power factor peaks at 0.968, and the driver deliberately folds output back outside its sweet spot instead of failing.

How the test was run

  • Input: 100–440 Vac, 50/60 Hz, stepped in 22 increments
  • Load: the full production LED string (24C6B for 100 W, 24C3B for 50 W)
  • Measured: input power, power factor, output voltage and current, efficiency, input current THD
  • Ambient: lab bench, 25–30 °C, after stabilisation at each step

Efficiency: flat where it matters

Between 165 Vac and 280 Vac — the range a real installation lives in — the 100 W high bay holds 89.1% to 92.6% and the 50 W flood light holds 86.6% to 92.4%. Both peak in the 260–280 Vac region, not at 230 Vac, because higher line voltage means lower input current and therefore lower conduction loss.

Power factor and THD move in opposite directions

Power factor climbs from 0.66 at 100 Vac to 0.968 at 270 Vac, then falls back to 0.815 at 440 Vac. THD does the mirror image: 110% at 100 Vac down to 22% at 280 Vac, then back up to 43% at 440 Vac. If your project is subject to a power-quality clause — and municipal street lighting almost always is — the number that matters is PF at the voltage you will actually be running at, not the headline figure on the front page of the datasheet.

The three zones you need to know about

This is the part that rarely appears in a datasheet. Reading the output-current curve, the driver has three distinct behaviours:

ZoneInput rangeBehaviourWhat it means for you
Low-line foldback100–160 VacOutput current folds back deliberately; drive power falls to a fraction of nominalThe luminaire dims instead of failing. Useful during deep sags and brownouts
Full regulation165–280 VacConstant current held at 610 mA (100 W) / 310 mA (50 W), ±1%This is where the luminaire is specified to perform. Design your lighting calculations here
High-line derating290–440 VacOutput current reduces progressively; at 440 Vac the driver still runs, at 38 WSurvives a sustained over-voltage event instead of failing. Plan for reduced output
The honest answer

The driver does not deliver 100 W at 440 Vac — nothing on this platform does, and any supplier claiming otherwise is either using a much larger magnetic core or not measuring. What it does deliver is survival with graceful derating, which is what a municipal installation actually needs during a 440 Vac swell.

What to ask a supplier before you accept the 100–440 Vac claim

  1. Can I see the output current curve, not just the input range on the cover?
  2. At what input voltage does full regulation start and end?
  3. What is the efficiency at the lowest and highest voltage in the range, not at 230 Vac?
  4. Is the surge rating measured at the extremes of the range, or only at nominal?
  5. What happens to output current during a sustained over-voltage — shutdown or derating?

A supplier who can answer all five has actually tested the range. One who cannot has copied a sentence.
